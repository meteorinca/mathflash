# Grade 4

## Place Value
**Why:** We use place value to tell us how big a digit is based on its position in a number.
**How:** Digits change their value based on where they sit. A 3 in the tens spot is worth 30; that same 3 in the hundreds is worth 300. Each hop left = ×10. Commas are just labels that say "a new group starts here": millions, thousands, ones.

## Rounding
**Why:** We don't always need the exact number — sometimes a close enough is good enough.
**How:** Find the place you're rounding to. Look at the digit to its right. 5 or more? Kick it up one. 4 or less? Let it rest. Everything after becomes zeros.

## Area
**Why:** When you run into a rectangle such as a field where you have to plant grass, you use area to figure out how many bags of grass seed you need to cover the whole space. All multiplication can be thought of as making a rectangle with rows and columns, so you can count how many squares fit inside.
**How:** For a rectangle, multiply length × width. Think of it as counting rows of squares: number of rows times squares in each row. Label in square units.

## Perimeter
**Why:** When you have to build a fence around a rectangular yard, you use perimeter to figure out how much fencing material you need to walk the entire boundary.
**How:** Add up all the side lengths. Every single side gets counted once. Label in regular units (not squares).

## Factors
**Why:** Some numbers are made up of factors. A factor is a number that divides another number evenly, with nothing left over. Factors are the "building blocks" that multiply together to make a number. You can think of finding factors by trying to make rectangles with tiles. The length of each row and the number of rows are the factors.
**How:** Think of the rectangle test. Can you make a rectangle with that many tiles using equal rows? The row length and number of rows are factors. List them in pairs so you don't miss any.

## GCF (Greatest Common Factor)
**Why:** We sometimes need to "simplify" fractions, or reduce them to the simplest form. This involves finding the greatest common factor of two numbers. The GCF is the largest factor that two numbers share.
**How:** List all the factors of each number. Circle the ones they both have. The largest circled number is the GCF. Start from the biggest possible and work down — faster that way.

## LCM (Least Common Multiple)
**Why:** We sometimes need to add fractions with different denominators. To do this, we need to find the least common multiple of the two denominators. The LCM is the smallest multiple that two numbers share.
**How:** Skip-count by the larger number. Stop at the first one the smaller number also hits. That's the LCM. No need to list everything.

## Prime Numbers
**Why:** Prime numbers are numbers that can only be divided by 1 and themselves. They are the building blocks of all other numbers.
**How:** A prime has exactly two factors: 1 and itself. That means 1 doesn't count — it only has one factor. Check: can you make a rectangle with it using more than one row? If not, it's prime.

## Composite Numbers
**Why:** Composite numbers are numbers that have more than two factors. They can be split up in different ways. Almost every number you meet is composite.
**How:** If you can build a rectangle with it that isn't just one long row, it's composite. Has at least three factors: 1, itself, and something else.

## Remainders
**Why:** When you divide numbers, you sometimes have leftover pieces that can't make a full group. The remainder tells you what's left.
**How:** Divide as far as you can go. Whatever doesn't fit into an equal group is the remainder. Check: remainder must be smaller than what you're dividing by. If not, you can make another group.

## Equivalent Fractions
**Why:** Fractions can have different names but the exact same value — like saying "half" or "two-fourths." Same amount of pizza, different way to slice it.
**How:** Multiply or divide both top and bottom by the same number. That's the one rule. Why it works: you're just changing the size of the pieces while changing how many you have, so the total amount stays identical. Drawing it helps.

## Adding Fractions (Like Denominators)
**Why:** When you add fractions with like denominators, you're just counting pieces of the same size. It's straightforward — you're just counting pieces.
**How:** Denominators match? Keep the denominator. Add the numerators. That's how many pieces you now have. Simplify if possible.

## Adding Fractions (Different Denominators)
**Why:** Sometimes you need to add fractions with different denominators. Since fractions just mean different sized slices of a whole, you can't add them until they have the same sized slices (same denominator).
**How (three steps — do these in order every time):**
1. **Find a common denominator.** Use the LCM of the two denominators. This will be your new bottom number.
2. **Fix the numerators.** Whatever you multiplied the bottom by, multiply the top by the exact same thing. Both fractions get renamed.
3. **Now add.** Denominators stay the same. Add the numerators. Simplify at the end if you can.


## Mixed Numbers
**Why:** Sometimes you have more than one whole plus a fraction leftover. The whole number tells you how many complete ones. The fraction tells you the extra part that didn't finish a whole.
**How:** To get from mixed number to improper fraction: multiply the whole number by the denominator and add the numerator. The denominator stays the same.

## Improper Fractions
**Why:** In a fraction: if the numerator is bigger than the denominator, you have more than one whole. An improper fraction is just a mixed number wearing a different outfit.
**How:** Divide the numerator by the denominator. The quotient is your whole number. The remainder becomes the numerator of the fraction part. Denominator stays the same.

## Decimals
**Why:** Decimals are another way to write fractions with denominators of 10, 100, 1000. The decimal point separates wholes from parts — everything to the right is less than one.
**How:** The first place after the decimal is tenths. Second is hundredths. Third is thousandths. Each hop right = ÷10. The pattern mirrors place value: just keep dividing by ten.

## Multiplying Fractions
**Why:** Multiplying by a fraction means you want a *piece of a piece*. It makes things smaller when the fraction is less than one — and that's normal.
**How:** Straight across. Numerator × numerator. Denominator × denominator. Simplify if you can. Drawing a rectangle split both ways shows exactly why this works: overlap is the product.

## Angles
**Why:** An angle measures how much one line has turned away from another. Think of opening a door — the angle is how wide the gap is.
**How:** Measured in degrees. A full turn is 360°. Key benchmarks: 90° is a square corner (right angle). 180° is a straight line. Less than 90° = acute (small and cute). Between 90° and 180° = obtuse (bigger, more open).

## Parallel & Perpendicular
**Why:** These words describe special relationships between lines. They help us classify shapes and build things that are strong and straight.
**How:** Parallel lines never cross — think railroad tracks, same distance apart forever. Perpendicular lines cross at exactly a right angle — think the corner of a book. That 90° intersection is the test.

## Measurement Conversions
**Why:** Different situations call for different units. We convert to switch between them without changing the actual amount.
**How:** Know your bridge numbers (12 inches = 1 foot, 3 feet = 1 yard, 60 minutes = 1 hour, etc.). Going to a bigger unit? Divide. Going to a smaller unit? Multiply. Your anchor: the bigger the unit, the fewer you need; the smaller the unit, the more you need.

## Symmetry
**Why:** A shape has symmetry if you can fold it along a line and both halves match perfectly — like a butterfly's wings.
**How:** The fold line is the line of symmetry. Some shapes have one, some have many, some have zero. Test it: fold in your mind (or on paper). Do both sides line up exactly? Then it's symmetrical.

# Grade 5

## Order of Operations
**Why:** When a problem has multiple operations mixed together, you need a shared set of rules so everyone gets the same answer. Without it, the same problem could have a dozen different answers.
**How:** PEMDAS — Parentheses first, then Exponents, then Multiply/Divide left to right, then Add/Subtract left to right. Think of it as a priority list: always knock out the highest-priority operation first before moving on.

## Exponents
**Why:** Repeated multiplication gets long to write out. Exponents are shorthand — 2⁵ means "multiply 2 by itself 5 times," not "2 times 5."
**How:** The base is what you're multiplying. The exponent tells you how many times. 3² = 3 × 3 = 9. 2³ = 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. Common mistake: 3² ≠ 6. The exponent is a count of copies, not a multiplier.

## Dividing Fractions
**Why:** Dividing by a fraction asks: "how many of this fraction fit inside that number?" It's the same idea as regular division, just with pieces instead of wholes.
**How:** Keep the first fraction, change ÷ to ×, flip the second fraction (take its reciprocal). Then multiply straight across. The phrase: Keep, Change, Flip. Always works. Always.

## Volume
**Why:** Area covers a flat surface. Volume fills a 3D space — think how much water fits in a box, or how much sand fills a container.
**How:** For a rectangular prism: length × width × height. You can also think of it as area of the base × height — you're just stacking layers. Label in cubic units (cm³, in³, ft³).

## Coordinate Plane
**Why:** We use a coordinate plane to describe exact locations in two directions at once — like giving someone GPS coordinates instead of vague directions.
**How:** The x-axis goes left/right. The y-axis goes up/down. A point like (3, 5) means: start at the origin, go 3 right, then 5 up. Always x first, then y. Remember: run before you rise — move horizontally first.

## Decimal Multiplication
**Why:** Sometimes you need to multiply amounts that aren't whole — like price times quantity when things cost $3.75. The answer should feel reasonable: multiplying decimals less than one makes the result smaller.
**How:** Ignore the decimal points and multiply like whole numbers. Then count the total decimal places in both factors. Put that many decimal places in your answer from the right. Double-check: does the size of your answer make sense?

## Decimal Division
**Why:** Dividing decimals comes up constantly — splitting a cost, finding a unit rate, converting measurements. The strategy is to make it look like whole-number division.
**How:** Move the decimal in the divisor all the way right to make it a whole number. Move the decimal in the dividend the exact same number of places. Now divide like normal. The decimal in your answer goes straight up from where it lands in the dividend.

## Comparing & Ordering Fractions
**Why:** Fractions look different but can mean the same thing — or one can be bigger without it being obvious. You need a fair way to compare them.
**How:** Make the denominators match (use LCM). Once they share a denominator, compare numerators directly — bigger numerator, bigger fraction. Or convert to decimals and compare. Both strategies work; pick the faster one.

## Graphing Data
**Why:** Raw numbers are hard to see patterns in. A graph turns data into a shape, and your brain is really good at reading shapes.
**How:** Line plots: each X marks one data point on a number line — good for showing spread and clusters. Bar graphs: height of each bar = frequency or value. Always label your axes and title your graph. Ask: what's the graph telling you at a glance?

## Powers of 10
**Why:** Our whole number system is built on 10s. Multiplying or dividing by powers of 10 is one of the most useful shortcuts in all of math.
**How:** Multiplying by 10ⁿ: move the decimal point n places to the right (number gets bigger). Dividing by 10ⁿ: move the decimal point n places to the left (number gets smaller). The decimal doesn't actually move — the digits shift relative to it.

# Grade 6

## Ratios
**Why:** A ratio compares two quantities and tells you their relationship. Ratios show up everywhere: recipes, maps, speed, prices. They let you scale things up or down while keeping the relationship the same.
**How:** Write as a:b, a/b, or "a to b" — all the same thing. To check if two ratios are equivalent, cross-multiply or simplify both to the same fraction. Think of it like a recipe: if the ratio is right, the result is right no matter how much you make.

## Unit Rates
**Why:** A unit rate tells you the amount per *one* of something — miles per hour, cost per item, words per minute. It makes comparing different-sized things fair.
**How:** Divide both numbers in the ratio so the second number becomes 1. The result is your unit rate. Example: 150 miles in 3 hours → 150 ÷ 3 = 50 miles per hour. Label it clearly with "per."

## Percentages
**Why:** Percent means "out of 100." It's a universal language for comparing parts of different-sized wholes — whether it's a test score, a discount, or a tax.
**How:** To find a percent of a number: convert the percent to a decimal (divide by 100), then multiply. 30% of 80 = 0.30 × 80 = 24. To find what percent one number is of another: divide the part by the whole, then multiply by 100.

## Integers
**Why:** Positive numbers only get you so far. Integers include negatives, which model debt, temperature below zero, floors underground — real situations where going below zero matters.
**How:** Integers are the whole numbers and their negatives: …−3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3… On a number line, left = smaller, right = larger. Absolute value = distance from zero, always positive. |−7| = 7.

## Adding & Subtracting Integers
**Why:** Combining values that can be positive or negative — like gains and losses, temperatures rising and falling — requires integer arithmetic.
**How:** Same signs? Add and keep the sign. Different signs? Subtract the smaller absolute value from the larger, then use the sign of the one with greater absolute value. Subtracting a negative is the same as adding a positive: 5 − (−3) = 5 + 3 = 8.

## Expressions & Variables
**Why:** Variables let you write a rule once that works for any number — like a recipe that scales. An expression is a math phrase; it doesn't have an equals sign. It describes a value, not a statement about it.
**How:** A variable (like x or n) stands in for an unknown or changing number. To evaluate an expression, substitute the value and simplify using order of operations. 3x + 2 when x = 5: 3(5) + 2 = 17.

## Equations
**Why:** An equation says two things are equal. Solving it means finding what value of the variable makes that true. It's like a balance — whatever you do to one side, you must do to the other.
**How:** Goal: get the variable alone. Use inverse operations — addition undoes subtraction, multiplication undoes division. Work one step at a time. Check your answer by plugging it back in. Both sides should be equal.

## Area of Triangles & Other Shapes
**Why:** Not everything is a rectangle. Triangles, parallelograms, and trapezoids all need their own formulas — but they're all connected back to rectangles underneath.
**How:** Triangle: ½ × base × height. The height must be perpendicular to the base — not the slanted side. Parallelogram: base × height (same idea, no fraction needed). Trapezoid: ½ × (base₁ + base₂) × height — average the two bases, then scale by height.

## Statistical Measures
**Why:** A list of numbers is hard to summarize. Mean, median, and mode each tell a different story about what's "typical" in a data set. Knowing which one to use is the real skill.
**How:** Mean: add all values, divide by count. Median: order the data, find the middle value (or average the two middle values). Mode: the value that appears most often. Range: largest minus smallest — tells you the spread, not the center.

## Nets & Surface Area
**Why:** Surface area tells you how much material covers the outside of a 3D shape — like the cardboard used to make a box. A net is what the shape looks like unfolded flat.
**How:** Unfold the shape in your mind (or on paper) into its faces. Find the area of each face. Add them all up. For a rectangular prism: there are 3 pairs of rectangles. Find each pair's area and double it, then add all three pairs together.
